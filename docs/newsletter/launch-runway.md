# Part 1 - The "One right you have" bank

Each is a self-contained ~110-word segment. Slot one into every weekly issue; each also
stands alone as a LinkedIn post, a carousel, or a short-video script. Lead with the
right, keep it plain, end with where to verify. **Verify each before publishing.**

**1. The 8am-9pm rule.** A debt collector generally can't contact you at a time they
know is inconvenient - and the default rule is no calls before 8am or after 9pm your
local time. If a collector is calling at 6am or 11pm, that's not a gray area. You can
tell them, ideally in writing, when and how they may contact you. (CFPB; FDCPA.)

**2. You can make them prove it.** Within 30 days of a collector's first contact, you can
send a written request to "validate" the debt. Until they do, they're supposed to pause
collection. Validation should show what you allegedly owe and to whom. Never assume a
debt is yours, the right amount, or still collectible just because someone called.
Putting the burden back on them is one of your strongest, cheapest moves. (CFPB; FDCPA.)

**3. "Stop contacting me" is a real switch.** You can tell a collector in writing to stop
contacting you. Once they receive it, they generally must stop - except to confirm
they'll stop, or to tell you about a specific action like a lawsuit. It doesn't erase
the debt, and it can prompt other steps, so use it deliberately. But the power to end the
phone calls is yours, in writing. (CFPB; FDCPA.)

**4. They can't drag your job into it.** A collector can't contact you at work once they
know your employer prohibits it - and you're allowed to tell them that. They also
generally can't discuss your debt with your boss or coworkers. Your debt is between you
and the creditor, not your workplace. (CFPB; FDCPA.)

**5. Harassment is illegal, not just rude.** Collectors can't threaten violence, use
obscene language, call repeatedly to annoy you, or falsely threaten arrest or actions
they can't or won't take. They can't pretend to be a lawyer, a government agency, or law
enforcement. If it feels like intimidation, write down the date, time, and what was said.
(CFPB; FDCPA.)

**6. They have to say who they are.** In communications, a collector generally must
identify that they're a debt collector and that information will be used for that
purpose. A caller who won't say which company they are, what the debt is, or who the
original creditor was is a red flag - and a reason to request validation in writing
before paying a cent. (CFPB; FDCPA.)

**7. Your credit report isn't carved in stone.** Under the FCRA, you can dispute
information you believe is wrong on your credit report, for free. The bureau generally
must investigate, usually within about 30 days, and correct or remove anything that
can't be verified. Errors on debt accounts are common; checking is free and costs you
nothing but a little time. (CFPB; FCRA.)

**8. Your credit reports are free.** You're entitled to free credit reports from the
three nationwide bureaus through the official source, annualcreditreport.com. Pulling
your own report does not hurt your score. You can't build a plan around debts you can't
see - reading your own report is step zero, and it's free. (CFPB; FCRA.)

**9. Old debt may be "time-barred."** Every state sets a statute of limitations - a
window after which a creditor generally can no longer sue you to collect. The clock and
rules vary by state and debt type, and certain actions (like making a payment or even
acknowledging the debt) can sometimes restart it. Before you pay or promise anything on
an old debt, it's worth knowing where your state's line is. (CFPB; FTC.) _[Founders:
confirm framing; do not give state-specific timelines without review.]_

**10. Forgiven debt can be taxed.** If a creditor cancels $600 or more of what you owe,
they may send a 1099-C, and the forgiven amount can count as taxable income. It's not a
reason to avoid settling - just a reason to go in with eyes open and, for larger
amounts, ask a tax professional. A "win" you didn't plan for can come with a smaller
bill in April. (IRS; general education - not tax advice.)

## Issue 1 - "The 60-minute head start"
**Subject:** The most useful hour you'll spend on your debt
**Preheader:** Not a payment. An inventory.

Hi {{first_name|there}},

When debt feels bottomless, it's usually because it's invisible - scattered across cards,
apps, and a vague number in your head. The first real move isn't a payment. It's making
it countable.

For one hour, list every debt in one place: who you owe, the balance, the interest rate,
and the minimum. That's it. People tell us this hour is the moment the panic drops a
notch, because "infinite" becomes a finite list you can actually work.

## Issue 2 - "Snowball vs avalanche, settled"
**Subject:** Which debt to attack first (the honest answer)
**Preheader:** Math vs momentum. Both are valid.

Hi {{first_name|there}},

Two methods, endless internet arguing. Here's the calm version.

**Avalanche:** pay extra toward your highest-interest debt first. It saves the most
money, mathematically. **Snowball:** pay off your smallest balance first. It saves less
on paper but gives you a win sooner, which keeps people going.

The best method is the one you'll actually stick with. If you're motivated by numbers,
avalanche. If you're motivated by momentum, snowball. There's no wrong answer that ends
with you debt-free.

**One right you have:** [Bank #2 - you can make them prove it.]

**This week's small step:** pick one method and point your next extra dollar at one debt.
See both timelines side by side here: https://www.detta.us/#calculator

## Issue 3 - "The hardship call"
**Subject:** What to actually say when you call your card company
**Preheader:** A framework, not a script to memorize.

Hi {{first_name|there}},

Calling your credit card company when you're behind is intimidating. A simple framework
helps more than a memorized script:

1. **Name it plainly:** "I've had a change in my finances and I want to stay current."
2. **Ask what exists:** "What hardship or payment programs do you offer?" Many issuers
   have them and don't advertise them.
3. **Get it in writing** before you agree to anything.
4. **Don't overshare or overpromise** a number you can't actually pay.

You're not asking for a favor. You're a customer trying to resolve an account - which is
exactly what they'd rather you do than default.

**One right you have:** [Bank #5 - harassment is illegal, not just rude.]

**This week's small step:** if one account is keeping you up at night, write down those
four lines before you call.

## Issue 4 - "Can they actually sue you?"
**Subject:** What can really happen if you fall behind
**Preheader:** The honest timeline, minus the scare tactics.

Hi {{first_name|there}},

Fear fills in the blanks with worst cases. Here's the calmer reality of how things
usually move, so you can plan instead of dread.

Falling behind first means late fees and credit-report marks. Accounts may move to
collections. A creditor *can* sue, but it takes a lawsuit and a court judgment before
anyone can garnish wages - it isn't instant, and it isn't automatic. Knowing the actual
sequence is what turns dread into a to-do list.

**One right you have:** [Bank #9 - old debt may be "time-barred."]

**This week's small step:** if you've been avoiding opening the mail, open it. Knowing
where each account stands is the whole game.
