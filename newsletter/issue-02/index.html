The Detta Letter · Issue #2

Relief arrived, and so did the fakes

November 23, 2026 · The state of the debt world, in plain English · General education, not legal advice

This issue in one breath: Two multistate settlements will erase hundreds of millions of dollars in consumer debt, regulators shut down operations that faked lawsuits and faked debt relief, and medical-debt protections grew in Maine and were enforced in Colorado while a court cut one back in Texas. Translation for you: real relief exists, so do people pretending to offer it, and your rights depend more than ever on where you live.

Story oneHundreds of millions in debt, wiped out

In September, a coalition of more than 40 state attorneys general reached a settlement with subprime auto lender Credit Acceptance. The company will "eliminate all debt owed by more than 55,000 consumers nationwide, resulting in more than $630 million in debt relief," according to the New York Attorney General. Minnesota's Attorney General says affected consumers do not need to take any action and will be contacted.

In November, a 43-state settlement with rent-to-own company Acima included $130 million in lease balance reductions. It also requires Acima to stop reporting rental-purchase agreements to the three big credit bureaus for five years, according to New Jersey's Attorney General. And in New York, debt buyers that the Attorney General says went after mortgage payments older than the state's six-year statute of limitations in foreclosure cases must drop those demands and pay more than $480,000. These settlements resolve the states' allegations; they are not court findings.

What it means for you: for the Credit Acceptance and Acima settlements, the Minnesota and New Jersey attorneys general say eligible consumers do not need to take any action and will be contacted. None of the releases describes a fee or an application. Be wary of anyone who calls offering to "claim" settlement relief for a fee. The New York case is also a reminder that a statute of limitations can apply even to debt tied to your home.

Story twoFake lawsuits, fake relief

California's financial regulator, the DFPI, ordered an unlicensed collector called Frontier Legal Group to stop. According to the September 8 order, the group mailed California consumers letters titled "Notification of Lawsuit & Civil Complaint" that carried a "FILED" stamp, accused them of "wire fraud," and threatened wage garnishment, liens, and bank levies. The regulator found that no legal action had ever been filed.

On the federal side, a federal court entered a $106.8 million judgment on October 14 against the companies behind "Accelerated Debt Settlement" and banned them from the debt-relief business. The FTC says the operation impersonated banks, credit card companies, and government agencies, claimed it could cut people's debt by as much as 75% or more, and collected illegal advance fees. The judgment against the companies was entered by default.

What it means for you: a "FILED" stamp on a letter does not prove you have been sued; real lawsuits come with a court summons, delivered the way your state's rules require. If a letter says you have been sued, look up the court's phone number yourself and ask the clerk whether a case exists. And a debt-relief company that wants money before it settles anything is a red flag: federal telemarketing rules generally prohibit those advance fees. More warning signs in our guide to debt collector scams.

Story threeMedical debt: more protection in some states, less in one

In Maine, a law barring liens on a person's primary home and wage garnishment to collect medical debt was signed in April and took effect on July 29, the general effective date for that session's laws. In Colorado, medical-debt collector Wakefield & Associates agreed to pay a $30,000 fine and to ask courts to undo default judgments that remain unpaid among 44 accounts where, the state says, it did not file the proof Colorado law requires before a default judgment on medical debt incurred since May 4, 2023. The order now requires an affidavit from the medical provider in those cases. The September order says it is not an admission of wrongdoing, and Wakefield denies liability.

Texas went the other way. On August 10, a federal court ruled that the federal credit reporting law overrides a 2019 Texas law that kept certain surprise medical bills off credit reports, and it barred the state from enforcing that law. And there is still no federal rule keeping medical debt off credit reports: a court vacated the CFPB's rule in July 2025.

What it means for you: your medical-debt rights now depend heavily on your state. The rules on what a collector must prove, whether it can garnish your pay, and what can appear on your credit report differ from place to place. Your state attorney general's website is a good place to start, and never ignore real court papers.

Also worth knowingStudent loans, the CFPB, and the Supreme Court

  • Forced student loan collections are still paused. As of today, Federal Student Aid says collections through Treasury offset (taking tax refunds) and Administrative Wage Garnishment "are currently paused." The page says it mostly applies to defaulted loans held by the Education Department, and it gives no restart date. On September 30, Treasury and Education opened an online support center where borrowers in default can apply to get out of default and make payments.
  • CFPB complaints still work, but the public stories are gone. On August 14 the CFPB stopped publishing consumers' written complaint stories in its public database. It says it will keep collecting and responding to complaints. In late September, a federal court in Oregon ordered the bureau's acting director to request the funding it needs from the Federal Reserve, according to Minnesota's Attorney General, one of the states that sued.
  • The Supreme Court took a bankruptcy case. On November 6, the Court agreed to hear Goldman Sachs Bank USA v. Brown. It asks whether people in bankruptcy can be sent to private arbitration when they sue a lender for continuing to collect after they filed. There is no decision yet.

By the numbersHousehold debt, third quarter 2026

From the New York Fed's November 10 report: total household debt rose $334 billion in the quarter to $19.1 trillion. Credit card balances rose $22 billion to $1.29 trillion. And 5.0% of all household debt was in some stage of delinquency, up 0.3 percentage points from the previous quarter. One brighter spot: the annualized rate of credit card balances newly becoming seriously late (90 days or more) was 6.87%, a little lower than the 7.05% a year earlier.

One right you haveYou can dispute errors on your credit report, free

With more debt falling behind, it is worth making sure what is reported about you is accurate. If something on your credit report is wrong, you can dispute it with the credit bureau for free. The bureau generally must finish its reinvestigation within 30 days of receiving your dispute, with up to 15 more days if you send additional relevant information during that time. If it finds the information inaccurate or incomplete, or it cannot be verified, the bureau must delete or correct it, and it must send you the results in writing within 5 business days of finishing. The CFPB advises explaining in writing what is wrong and why, with copies of documents that support your dispute. One caveat: a bureau does not have to investigate a dispute it reasonably decides is frivolous or irrelevant, though it must tell you so, and why, within 5 business days. This is a right you use yourself, and no one can legally remove information that is accurate and current (FTC). (FCRA, 15 U.S.C. § 1681i; CFPB.)
